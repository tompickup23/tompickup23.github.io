Method, sources and legal

How this is built

Every figure on the Observatory can be traced to a public source and a date. This page explains what the Observatory is, how each derived measure is worked out and what it does not mean, where the data comes from and under what licence, and how to get something corrected.

What this is

This is a journalistic publication on the use of public money in Lancashire, published in the public interest.

It brings together information that is already public by law: the company register, insolvency notices, food hygiene ratings, official statistics and councils' own spending data. Company profiles sit inside analysis of the local economy; there is deliberately no directory or "search all directors" feature. Scores and indicators are opinions formed from the public facts by the published method below, shown next to those facts, never presented as statements of fact about any company's solvency, creditworthiness or probity.

Geography: the two views

Lancashire is mid-reorganisation. Every area is therefore shown in two ways, and a toggle switches between them:

  • Councils today The twelve districts of Lancashire County Council plus the two existing unitary authorities, Blackpool and Blackburn with Darwen: fourteen areas.
  • Four unitaries (2028) The four new councils that take over on 1 April 2028: North Lancashire, West Lancashire, East Lancashire and South Lancashire. Each area's figures roll up into its new unitary.

One naming point, because it catches people out: the 2028 West Lancashire unitary is Blackpool, Fylde and Wyre on the coast. It is not the current West Lancashire district, which joins South Lancashire. To keep the two apart, the current district uses the address /lancs/business/west-lancashire/ and the new coastal unitary uses /lancs/business/west-lancashire-unitary/.

The clean distress indicator

The distress indicator is an Observatory assessment, not a statement of fact about any company. For an area, it is the share of active companies showing register-based stress signals (for example an active strike-off proposal, overdue accounts, or a filed insolvency step), measured against the active company base.

The clean rate excludes companies registered at flagged address clusters: single addresses where an unusually large number of companies are registered and most are dissolved or striking off. Those clusters can sit in one postcode and would otherwise distort a whole area's figure, so they are set aside and the raw (pre-exclusion) rate is shown alongside on each area profile. Cluster notes describe only the observable pattern, for example "411 companies registered at this address; 98 percent dissolved or in strike-off". They never assert a purpose.

Each figure carries its basis and its data date. A company-level signal is shown next to the underlying register fact it comes from, so the reader sees the fact and the assessment together.

The high-growth (gazelle) assessment

Two measures sit on the growth page. The official benchmark is the ONS Business Demography high-growth share: companies averaging 20 percent annual growth in employment over three years, from a base of ten or more employees. That is a published official statistic, shown per area against the England share for the same year.

The candidate scale-ups are an Observatory assessment, our own read of the average employee numbers disclosed in filed accounts (section 411, Companies Act 2006) over at least three consecutive periods. It is not turnover-based and has not been checked with the companies. Only complete series from a base of ten or more employees carry the "meets ONS definition" flag; a base of three to nine with strong growth is flagged "emerging" and shown separately. Obvious data errors (for example a ten-fold jump from a filing typo) are cleaned out, as is any figure that cannot be a headcount at all. Where a company has filed more than once for the same accounting period, the figures shown are the ones in the most recently filed set of accounts for that period, so a restatement replaces the original filing. Every candidate states its basis in the table.

The two measures count different universes and will not match. The official share is built from the Inter-Departmental Business Register, which covers businesses that never disclose employee numbers at Companies House; the candidate list is built only from filed accounts. The benchmark says how much high growth an area has; the candidate list says which filing companies show it. High growth is also a period, not a type of company: most firms that meet the definition in one three-year window do not meet it in the next, so the list is a snapshot of the window shown, never a forecast.

The Lancashire Pound tiers

Each council's published payments to suppliers are sorted into four tiers, an Observatory assessment based on register facts:

  • Rooted local Registered office and trading premises in Lancashire, with no controlling parent registered outside the county.
  • Trades here, owned away Trades from Lancashire premises, but the controlling parent is registered outside Lancashire. Described as "revenue flows to owners registered outside Lancashire", never as "siphoning".
  • Non-local No registered office or trading premises identified in Lancashire.
  • Unclassified Not enough register evidence to place the supplier.

Which parent the ownership chain names. A company can have more than one corporate person with significant control on the register at the same time, and about one in nine of those that have any have two or more. Where that happens, every controller's chain is walked, and the company takes the tier of its least local controller: a supplier is described as rooted only if every one of its registered corporate controllers resolves to Lancashire ownership. The chain shown is the controller that sets the tier, so the basis for the classification is always the chain on the page; where controllers tie, the one giving its own registered company number is preferred, then the lowest company number, which is the longest established. Each chain is walked upwards until it reaches a controller with no registered corporate owner of its own, and the register entry for the company lists all its controllers.

The basis for each supplier's tier is stated in register terms. The spend itself is each council's own published "payments over £500" transparency data. That data covers payments over £500 and is not a complete account of any council's budget: the numerator and denominator, and this caveat, are shown with the figures.

The modelled unregistered layer

Registered companies and registered unincorporated businesses are counts. The unregistered / below-threshold layer on each area profile is a modelled estimate, not a count, and is labelled as such wherever it appears. It applies a Business Population Estimates North West ratio to Annual Population Survey self-employment, to indicate activity that sits below registration thresholds. It must not be read as a headcount of businesses.

How a website gets onto a company profile

A company profile links to that company's website only when the site itself proves the match. There is no "probable" or "likely" tier, and nothing is published on a guess. Two proofs are accepted:

  • Registration number on the site The company's registration number appears on the page. UK companies must display it, so this is definitive.
  • Registered name and registered postcode The exact registered company name and the registered-office postcode both appear on the same page.

Candidate addresses come from register-published URLs (the Charity Commission extract), from the company's registered and trading names, and from a web search. Every candidate then has to pass the test above; anything that does not is discarded, along with everything fetched from it. A candidate that came from a search is held to the stricter of the two proofs, the registration number, because a page that quotes a company's name and address is not necessarily that company's own page. The quoted evidence is shown next to each link, with the date it was checked, and links are re-checked monthly so dead ones drop off.

Because the rule is strict, plenty of real websites are missed. Micro-firms in particular often do not display their registration number, and some sites block automated checks. A company with no link here is not a company without a website. As at the current edition, 273 of 1046 company profiles (26.1 percent) carry a verified link: 140 matched on the registration number and 133 on the registered name with the registered postcode.

The crawler that does this identifies itself, honours robots.txt, waits a second between requests to the same site and gives up on a site after five failures. It keeps the homepage text of verified sites only, to classify what a company actually does in a later edition. Nothing is retained from a site that fails the test.

Data dates

A register is a snapshot. Every file records when it was generated and when its sources were retrieved, and pages show a data-date line. A company figure reads, in effect, "based on accounts to a stated date, filed on a stated date, retrieved on a stated date". Figures reflect the register at the retrieval date shown and may have changed since. Food hygiene ratings, which can carry consumer-protection weight, are shown with their "as at" date and refreshed on a schedule.

Corrections policy

If something here is wrong, it gets fixed. Report it to tom.pickup@lancashire.gov.uk.

  • Acknowledge within 48 hours. Every report is acknowledged within two days.
  • Re-verify at the primary source. The claim is checked at the original register, never amended on a complainant's say-so alone.
  • Correct within 5 working days. Where a figure is wrong it is corrected within five working days and recorded in a public corrections log.
  • Right of reply. Where the figure is right but a company disputes it, the profile is annotated with the company's response.

Named individuals: privacy and objection route

Register data about directors and persons with significant control is personal data. It is published here for journalism in the public interest (the special-purposes basis under the Data Protection Act 2018), inside analysis of public money, not as a people-search tool. The lawful basis is legitimate interests, assessed and recorded before publication.

If you are named and want an entry reviewed, write to tom.pickup@lancashire.gov.uk. Objections are answered within one month. As a default, resigned officers of dissolved companies and individuals with no live public-money connection are suppressed on request; officers and persons with significant control of live companies holding public contracts are, as a default, retained, with a recorded balancing test, because of the public-money interest. No marketing or contact list is built from this data, and registered proprietors are never contacted.
